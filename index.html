Twelve weeks of tracking now span a six-phase regime arc — is the code any good? → is the agent safe? → is the harness right? → who governs the gate? → who pays the review cost? → and as of this week, who pays the cognitive cost of being unable to work without? Four signals reach promotion threshold simultaneously and a new behavioral-lock-in story (`ai-dependency-trap`) mints — the program's first window where the dominant question is not about the technology, but about the developers who have learned to depend on it.
This week’s big story
Coders refuse to work without AI — and the bill is coming due
A research team that runs productivity experiments on software developers tried to repeat their 2025 baseline study this month, and could not recruit anyone — developers refused to work without AI even briefly, according to a TechCrunch report on the METR experiment. The same piece confirms two companies have already paid for that refusal: Amazon quietly shut down its internal "Kirorank" leaderboard after employees gamed it by running up agent costs, and Uber burned through its 2026 AI budget in four months without measurable productivity gains.
The behavioral lock-in lands on top of Anthropic's own research showing developers using AI score 17% lower on comprehension tests than those coding manually, with productivity gains not reaching statistical significance. Meanwhile Thoughtworks Technology Radar v34 gave the problem a name — "codebase cognitive debt" — and put it on Trial, the analyst equivalent of "we are now tracking this institutionally."
The productivity case for AI coding has assumed developers can choose to use it. This week is the first time the analyst record has the data showing they can't, and the bill is showing up as cost-overruns rather than performance wins. Watch for: whether the GitHub Copilot June 1 usage-based billing cutover triggers a measurable practitioner backlash in the post-cutover week.
